Some thoughts on life through some collected writings

Some random passing thoughts on life.

April 20, 2026   ·   7 min read

#Philosophy #Literature #Personal

Around the age of 16, I was particularly angsty, especially with the uncertainty of the future ahead, about the person I wanted to be, what the meaning of life was/my own purpose, and what living a meaningful life looks like. It was around the same time I started to read a lot more history and classics, really whatever I could get my hands on to understand the world. This happened especially after reading Tennyson’s Ulysses and the line “to strike, to seek, to find, and not to yield”, and I attempted to seek out what was right for me. I was attempting to define the person I was and wished to be. It was a great time for me to really enjoy all these subjects I was learning about, but I also had no idea what I wanted to do, and I became extremely frustrated at myself.

Despite this, I knew some kind of power literature held. Millions of authors writing something as they themselves reach out into the abyss and yell into the void, making attempts to communicate and understand life. “An attempt!” they think, hoping their words would grasp onto someone and cling onto them as they go on with their one life. I think this is what ultimately lead me to write my high school personal statement: [[My Undergraduate Personal Statement]].

Side note, the wonderful Dean Sarah Zearfoss once wrote about the funny little thrill one has when they reread something they’ve written in the past, instead of finding it revolting or shameful. I will admit I feel now that my personal statement is quite tedious and lacking any personal anecdote with unnecessary details, although it is a gentle reflection of the person I was hopeful about being at tender age of 17.

As senior year came to a close, I’m in contact with Jay Kogen, an accomplished former writer for the Simpsons and Frasier, since I was writing comedy at that time. He lead me to Rabbi Twerski’s talk on finding purpose. I don’t think it had any religious tones, but I did pull away some thoughts, with them being: 1. We all have value and are worthy 2. Things with value and worth are often in two categories, aesthetics and functional. As most of us are not models, we are the latter. 3. In order to develop a stronger sense of self-value/worth, we must find what our function is 4. By seeking and striving for a purpose, we ultimately find it and along our way, develop our character, skills, and philosophy of life.

Listening to that really changed the way I thought abt how approaching the whole thing. I started to wonder to myself what could I do that would be functional and dedicated to a purpose that was greater than just serving myself. Poetry and literature were some of the ways I was able to define my purpose in greater detail. Like this Mary Oliver poem:

The Summer Day Who made the world? Who made the swan, and the black bear? Who made the grasshopper? This grasshopper, I mean– the one who has flung herself out of the grass, the one who is eating sugar out of my hands, who is moving her jaws back and forth instead of up, who is gazing around with her enormous and complicated eyes. Now she lifts her pale forearms and thoroughly washes her face. Now she snaps her wings open, and floats away. I don’t know exactly what a prayer is. I do know how to pay attention, how to fall down into the grass, how to kneel down in the grass how to be idle and blessed, how to stroll through the fields, which is what I have been doing all day. Tell me, what else should I have done? Doesn’t everything die at last, and too soon? Tell me, what is it you plan to do with your one wild and precious life?

Or this wonderful eulogy by President Obama for Ted Kennedy: “What we can do is to live out our lives as best we can with purpose, and love, and joy. We can use each day to show those who are closest to us how much we care about them, and treat others with the kindness and respect that we wish for ourselves. We can learn from our mistakes and grow from our failures. And we can strive at all costs to make a better world, so that someday, if we are blessed with the chance to look back on our time here, we can know that we spent it well; that we made a difference; that our fleeting presence had a lasting impact on the lives of other human beings.”

Part of the reason why I enjoy so much history is the realization of how

Other than that, George Bernard Shaw has this great quote from the Man and Superman. It goes

*“This is the true joy in life, the being used for a purpose recognized by yourself as a mighty one; the being a force of nature instead of a feverish, selfish little clod of ailments and grievances complaining that the world will not devote itself to making you happy.

I am of the opinion that my life belongs to the whole community, and as long as I live it is my privilege to do for it whatever I can. I want to be thoroughly used up when I die, for the harder I work the more I live. I rejoice in life for its own sake. Life is no “brief candle” for me. It is a sort of splendid torch which I have got hold of for the moment, and I want to make it burn as brightly as possible before handing it on to future generations.”*

When I first read this so many years ago, I felt like this was EXACTLY what I wanted to express throughout my whole life. My sister was a very precocious kid when she was little, and would often ask me questions like “why do we all live if we’re gonna die’. My response at that age often veered towards the idea of doing the best with my one life to give the opportunity for the next generations to experience the gift that is life.

So what is so special about life? I think often back to Proust’s In Search of Lost Time. His main point that life isn’t about climbing the social ladder (as we know from Tolstoy and his short story the Death of Ivan Ilyich) or fame or money or love. It is art. The takeaway is that we must view life with the same generosity as an artist. To most people, the details of life are blurry, insignificant, which becomes habit and limits us from appreciating the beauty that is life. To an artist’s eyes, the little details do matter and keeps the world alive and fresh. From the way the leaves of a tree breathe through it’s spectacles, or the trivialness of the taste of water, or the way our friends are there for us, or the way music passing through some cells could result in something so pleasurable, or the taste of a madeleine with a couple of tea, or the fact we exist at all with a degree of consciousness. The paintings of haystacks or buildings or portraits communicate in a similar way as they strip away habit and allow us to appreciate what this world is and has to offer, from whatever time it is at.

It is the complexity, the charm of the everyday and the world that makes life so special. And what a gift it is to impart that onto others and to work in a field that provides others with the opportunity for that.

That is the purpose I wish to fulfill and the reason I live.
