Glutamate Dehydrogenase Enzyme Report

An report on the metabolic enzyme glutamate dehydrogenase!

April 30, 2025   ·   14 min read

#Biology

Introduction #

Glutamate dehydrogenase (GLDH) is an enzyme localized within the mitochondrial matrix, both in prokaryotes and eukaryotes (1). GLDH catalyzes the reversible reaction between L-glutamate and a-ketoglutarate and ammonia while reducing NADP+ to NADPH in either catabolic or anabolic reactions (Figure 1A) (1,2). When α-ketoglutarate is produced, it can enter the Krebs cycle for further energy metabolism. While the reaction GLDH catalysis is reversible, mammals typically favor the forward reaction to produce a-ketoglutarate due to equilibrium effects (1). The reverse reaction is often observed in plants and fungi, where ammonia is not as toxic and the GLDH works in either direction depending on the environment and stress experienced (3,4). This paper will look into the active and inactive forms of GLDH in a black-mold Aspergillus niger belonging to the Fungi kingdom, while exploring the catalytic residues that are in the active site.

Across almost all species, the catalytic residues of glutamate dehydrogenase are conserved. The two main residues that participate in the catalytic reaction are Lysine and Aspartic acid (Figure 1B, Figure S1). The presence of the cofactor NADP+ and the addition of water is also required for catalytic activity. The active site is also sterically selective; literature from 1970s found that D-glutamate has been found to inhibit the activity of GLDH in fungus (5,6). First, Asp functions as a general base by deprotonating the amine group of L-glutamate. This results in the hydride transfer to NADP+, resulting in the formation of an iminoglutamate intermediate. Lys then preps a water molecule for a nucleophilic attack on the imine intermediate, and Asp transfers a proton to the amine. Left with a negatively charged carbonyl group, Asp then takes the proton off the hydroxyl group of the carbinolamine intermediate. This intermediate is unstable and immediately collapses, eliminating the ammonia while the former hydroxyl group becomes a keto. This generates the final products of a-ketoglutarate, alongside NADPH and the aforementioned ammonia. Although not pictured, Asp and Lys are later deprotonated by water to regenerate the active site to process another L-glutamate. Other residues can offer stabilizing interactions to place L-glutamate in the correct position for deamination.

REFERNCE FIGURE 5XVI: Crystal Structure of Aspergillus niger Apo2-Glutamate Dehydrogenase (Prakash et al., 2018) #

This structure by Prakash et al. was selected since it would enable exploration of inactive GLDH without any substrates in its active site, allow a clear view of the catalytic residues. The structure does not contain any mutations as well (Figure 2B). This structure was solved using x-ray crystallography and the source of this enzyme is from Aspergillus niger, a type of black mold belonging to the Fungi kingdom.

The authors achieved a 2.80 A resolution with this structure. This is a moderate resolution, meaning that it is good enough to resolve main-chain atoms and most of the side chains, but finer details like hydrogen bonding are less reliable. In this resolution, R-values in the range of 0.20 and 0.35 and an R-free value of 0.28-0.30 are typical. For this structure, the authors reported an observed R-value observed of 0.230, suggesting a decent fit between the model and the data. The R-free value was found to be high at a value of 0.310 and this is unideal, suggesting that the model may be overfitted or that certain regions are not well modeled. This could be due to disordered areas in the structure or the flexible nature of the crystal. The gap of 0.08 between the R-value observed and R-free value reinforces the idea of overfitting the data, perhaps not capturing the true structure. The model shows strong stereochemical quality with a low clash score of 3, indicating minimal steric interference between atoms. The percentage of Ramachandran outliers (0.7%) and sidechain outliers (4.5%) are somewhat elevated, suggesting potential misfitting or uncertainty in certain regions. This means that some regions might be experiencing poor electron density, and this uncertainty leads to perhaps incorrect fitting and refinement of those residues. However, the very low RSRZ outlier percentage (0.3%) indicates that most residues are well supported by electron density, reinforcing the overall reliability of the model despite a few local issues. The authors suggested that the high R-factors were due to the lower resolution and the poor redundancy of the diffraction data.

The quaternary structure of GLDH in this example is a homohexamer, meaning that all subunits are the same sequence, although they may have changes in conformation and folding (Figure 2A). Literature has suggested that the subunits are stabilized in their quaternary structure through hydrogen bonding and van der Waal interactions (9). Each subunit contains two domains: Domain I (residues 1-190 and 437-460) and Domain II (residue 191-436). The domains are made up of alpha helices and beta sheets (4). Domain I has roles in subunit assembly and is the main domain to engage in the binding of L-glutamate (Figure 2B). Domain II facilitates the binding of NADP+ and contains modified Rossman folds in two motifs of alternating alpha helices and beta strands (Figure 2C).

5XVX: Crystal Structure of Aspergillus niger Glutamate Dehydrogenase Complexed With Alpha-ketoglutarate and NADPH (Prakash et al., 2018) #

As mentioned before, fungi GLDH tend to favor the reverse reaction in that a-ketoglutarate along with NADPH goes onto produce L-glutamate. This catalytic reaction mechanism is detailed by the authors as well (Figure 3). As seen in this reaction mechanism, it is not very different from the forward reaction mechanism. Several other residues are observed as well, although they mostly play a stabilizing role for the substrates via hydrogen bonding. NADPH is also observed to nicely fit within the active site.

![[GDF3.jpg]] Figure 3 (Left): Catalytic reaction mechanism for the reductive amination of a-ketoglutarate (Prakash et al., 2018). The catalytic process begins with Asp-154 acting as a base to deprotonate NH4+, enabling ammonia to perform a nucleophilic attack on the a-carbon of a-ketoglutarate. This results in the formation of an oxyanion intermediate, which is protonated by a nearby water molecule positioned close to Lys-114. The proton transfer regenerates the water, and this tetrahedral intermediate is stabilized by the enzyme’s active site. Water is eliminated, resulting in the formation of a-iminoglutarate. Finally, a hydride is transferred from NADPH to the imine, yielding L-glutamate.

In PDB structure 5XVX solved by Prakash et al, GLDH is complexed with a-ketoglutarate (substrate) and NADPH (cofactor). This structure was selected because it shows the active form of GLDH with substrates inside its active site, allowing us to observe the catalytic residues at work. This structure is also only of Subunit I (Figure 4, 6). Since this is the same paper as the previous structure, the source organism is Aspergillus niger, and the structure was solved via x-ray crystallography.

The quality of structure 5XVX represents a substantial improvement compared to the previous model. The authors resolved the structure to a high resolution of 1.80 Å, enabling detailed visualization of atomic positions. An observed R-value of 0.136 and an R-free of 0.148 indicate a very strong agreement between the model and the experimental data, with minimal evidence of overfitting. Structural validation metrics further support the robustness of the model: the clashscore is low at 3, Ramachandran outliers are minimal at 0.2%, and sidechain outliers are only 0.8%. Additionally, the RSRZ outlier percentage stands at just 1.1%, confirming that nearly all residues are well supported by the electron density. Overall, the 5XVX structure demonstrates high stereochemical quality and accurate modeling, and these metrics suggest that the majority of all residues are well supported by the electron density (Figure 5, 8). The one exception is that ring structures do not have a gap in the middle, suggesting that the electron density map is not that well refined, but overall, all sidechains seem to be supported in the map. These were observed with a phenylalanine and the rings of NADPH.

This time around, a direct comparison between the inactive and active forms of GLDH can be observed. The inactive form shows the two domains of Subunit I in a more relaxed position (Figure 6A). While catalytic residues (K114, D154) still face the center quite boldly, stabilizing residues sidechains do not necessarily face all the same direction (Q99, K102, N346, R82, G153). However, in the presence of a-ketoglutarate and NADPH, these stabilizing residues have sidechains that all reach out to a-ketoglutarate. This strongly indicates the influence of hydrogen bonding between the stabilizing residues and a-ketoglutarate. The active form also shows the two domains of the subunit in a tighter position than before (Figure 6B).

Subunit I has been isolated in these two images. Domains are colored by domain I in teal and domain II is in magenta. Stabilizing residues are in green while catalytic residues are in yellow (Lys 114 and Asp 154). (A) Active sites are empty, and the enzyme is inactive. (B) Active site contains a-ketoglutarate in pale blue and NADPH in orange.

The active site is then further observed in the enzymes structure with a-ketoglutarate and NADPH (Figure 7A). Given how a carbon carbon bond is 1.5 A, the distance between the catalytic residues and a-ketoglutarate is not too far, averaging around 3.5 A (Figure 7B). This is also observed with the stabilizing residues, and a similar distance of hydrogen bond interactions was observed to average around 3.5 A between amine groups on the stabilizing residues and oxygen groups on a-ketoglutarate (Figure 7C). This suggests that in addition to residues that catalyze the reaction in GLDH, there must be stabilizing residues within other forms of GLDH that hold the substrate in place, as well as NADPH for hydride transferring. On the catalytic side, the close distance between the keto group on the a-ketoglutarate and the amine groups of the catalytic residues reinforces the idea that they are critical for the reductive amination of α-ketoglutarate in GLDH.

Multiple Sequence Alignment #

A multiple sequence alignment was performed using the Aspergillus niger protein (UniProt ID: B6V7E4) as the query. The search was conducted via NCBI BLAST against the UniProtKB database, yielding 73 homologous sequences. Sequence identity among the hits ranged from 23.51% to 84.85%, with query coverage between 68% and 100%. The resulting sequences were aligned in Jalview using the MUSCLE algorithm with default parameters. Visualization settings included the Gecos Flowers color scheme, and a conservation threshold set at 30%. Alignment images were subsequently generated and recorded.

GLDH has many conserved regions throughout all species. In particular, catalytic residues Lys (position 223) and Asp (position 266) were found to be conserved throughout all species yielded from BLAST sequence search. These catalytic residues are highlighted in red (Figure 9). These results suggest that catalytic residues Lys and Asp are conserved in GLDH.
