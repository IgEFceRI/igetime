Jazz notes from just a listener

Jazz notes from a listener

August 23, 2025   ·   5 min read

#Music #History #Personal #Jazz

Some of my favorite jazz albums are simply too good not to share. So, I might as well do that here. https://open.spotify.com/playlist/1NzZLTfGtzPJG3fUl9R7xY?si=8cb6a98b2e924c82

The major note for playlist is that hard-core or even causal listeners might not appreciate the selected albums as much–they are simply what I suggest anytime someone mentions casual interest in the genre. They are what I consider to be the kind of essential-non-lyrical jazz that you typically find in swing music. There are some unique finds and also many many wonderful creative amendments on standards.

I like to think of standards in a similar way to classical music. The responsibility of a classical musician, in my opinion, is to take an already written-piece and to interpret it to convey some sort of feeling or story. A jazz musician has a very similar responsibility, although the difference is how they do it. While a classical musician might change the dynamics or texture of the piece, but fidelity to the text is essential. Jazz is beyond an affair when it comes to being faithful for the text. Instead, it’s a wonderfully creative endeavor that practically turns it into a unique version of that standard every single time. That is anytime jazz happens, it’s a perfectly unique version for your ears and for this moment. Miles Davis’s “My funny valentine” is completely other than Gerry Mulligan.

If you are musically inclined, a wonderful way to look at the bare bones of the standard is flipping through a “fake book”. These include all sorts of types of standards, each of them known as a lead sheet or “fake sheets” (composed of a melody, lyrics, and harmony). The name of this rather funny phase of “fake sheets” and “fake books” comes from the idea that a proficient musician can improvise upon these without a full score that details how the piece should be played.

Onto the playlist. The way I explain through my choices might be out of order form what appears on the playlist, but I have reasons as to why I wish to explain them…

Dave Brubeck Quartet #

I think my first love in jazz was Take Five. I had not realized how fun a simple 5 count piece could be. It was something beyond the themes and variations I had paid with classical music. It was not exactly major or minor, but something between and mysterious. I included the album Time Out because it features classics with a new twist, such as Blue Rondo a la Turk. The whole album should be listened to as a whole.

I also happen to be a big fan of Paul Desmond. My favorite rendition of Emily is by him from 1970. It’s just so wistful and simple. Desmond also was rather found of Bach, and it’s always fun trying to find the motifs he hides in his solos.

Chet Baker #

Chet Baker Sings is a great introduction to the genre in general. His versions of the standards are less like the pop-music character of swing music (definitely less upbeat). However, because there are lyrics, it’s very easy to follow long, and every now and then, Chet will do a wonderful little solo.

Some favorites:

  1. I Fall in Love Too Easily
  2. My Buddy
  3. There Will Never be Another You

Miles Davis #

Pretty much every site, every article will mention Kind of Blue as the absolute must listen. I say, not so much. The problem with Kind of Blue is that it plunges average person into the deep end of jazz. There’s a lot going on, there’s people talking at the beginning about who’s going first(?), and should you even listen to the legacy edition or just the plain album? It’s totally overwhelming! A more gentle introduction is Relaxin’ With The Miles Davis Quintet and Workin’ With The Miles Davis Quintet. You can hear each of the instruments while being surround with familiar tunes. Comparing with Chet Baker Singers, you’ll notice the unique blend of sound, as well as incredible improvisational skills of the members of the band.

Lee Morgan #

It’s fun, it’s jazzy, it’s one of most recognizable trumpeters of all time, it’s Lee Morgan! His tone is so prominent, it’s the first thing you notice when you listen to Cornbead and it’s the last thing you think about when the album ends. Some of his phrasing reminds you of a conversation you’ve heard somewhere, but can’t describe. My favorites are Ceora and Most like Lee.

Red Garland #

There’s no jazz pianist like Red Garland that makes you appreciate the simplicity of his technique. It’s not flashy, it’s not playing with crazy chords. It’s just a man who understands the fundamentals of jazz so so well and understands how to use it. Blues in Mambo is my favorite album, and I love Estrellita. It’s so beautiful, somewhat blue, and so warm.

List of some other favorites #

  1. Gerry Mulligan sextet
    1. Tell me when: just so beautiful. It’s more simpler than Lee Morgan, although the phrasing is so slow and makes you appreciate the placement of each note.
    2. Night Lights (1965): similar to tell me when. I think that’s particularly why I enjoy this album so much. It takes you back to a time where maybe there’s an air of loneliness. Your mind is recalling to a time of bittersweetness.
  2. Charlie Parker Jam Sessions (Album) 1. Welcome to the land of what true creativity looks like! Despite listening to this album a million times, there are still part of the solos that surprise me. What is this time called love? is the perfect example of such of creativity. It’s not simple like Red Garland and you are figuring it out along with the musicians at what’s coming next.

Final Thoughts #

There are plenty more that I didn’t mention, although this is the basics of basics I thought I would go into slightly more detail than “it’s just really good.” I might go slightly more into Paul Desmond one of these days, as his music is what bought me to listening to more jazz. Until then, don’t forget the lick.
